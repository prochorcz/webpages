Feasibility Studies for the New Project Wave

Immediate hands-on help on the projects that are starting now — and, growing out of that work, one solid way of doing feasibility studies at VigRe for good. Two streams, one goal.

Discussion draft — for internal review
01 · Situation & goals

A wave of projects is starting — and feasibility can't keep up

A lot of new projects are heading for the starting line. Entering the delivery phase without a quality feasibility study puts the investment at risk: gaps in scope, cost, capacity and risk surface only when changing course is already expensive.

This matters because the feasibility stage is the last inexpensive decision point: the one moment where an initiative can still be confirmed, reshaped or stopped at the cost of a study rather than the cost of delivery. Every study done well now protects money and prepares a cleaner delivery start.

Two goals, served together

1 · The projects starting now get feasibility studies in decision quality

Concrete, immediate help where the pressure is: hands-on support on selected projects at the start of the wave — no lengthy analysis phase before help arrives.

2 · VigRe builds a lasting capability to do this itself

Out of the real work grows one shared standard: what a feasibility study contains, who does what, how the decision gate works — so every future study is done uniformly, by VigRe's own teams.

Longer-term outlook: once studies follow one standard, they become comparable across initiatives — which over future planning cycles enables better portfolio prioritisation and more informed allocation of funds. No change to the current planning process is required for this proposal; the foundation simply builds up as a by-product.
02 · The approach

Two streams, one system

We don't start with a methodology binder. We start in the field — helping selected starting projects produce their feasibility studies — and we let the standard grow out of what that work teaches us. Stream 1 delivers value from week one; Stream 2 follows in its slipstream and turns the lessons into a lasting, scalable practice.

Stream 1 · starts immediately

Hands-on feasibility work on live projects

Working inside the teams of 1–2 selected projects at the start of the wave — sleeves rolled up, sharing the workload of producing the study.

  • Frame what each study must answer
  • Facilitate the expert workshops & interviews
  • Carry the pen — structure, draft, write
  • Challenge assumptions, hunt for gaps
  • Prepare decision-quality gate materials
Outputs Feasibility studies ready for decision — plus a gap log: a structured record of where today's practice diverges from good practice.

Stream 2 · in the slipstream

One standard, owned by VigRe

Starting from a joint review of the gap log after the first pilot study — codifying what proved to work into VigRe's own standard.

  • Feasibility stage definition — what it is / is not
  • Dimensions every study must examine
  • Gate model, roles & decision rights
  • Templates: study, decision paper, checklists

03 · Stream 1 in detail

Hands in the dirt, not an advisory board

This is not a review committee and not remote quality assurance. Our consultant works inside the project team as extra capacity and extra know-how: running the sessions, holding the structure, writing the document, asking the uncomfortable questions — so the study gets done, and done well, without exhausting the team.

Framing the assignment

With the sponsor and the team we pin down what exactly the study must answer, which options are on the table, who the experts are, and the study's own plan — the step where most studies silently go wrong.

Carrying the workload

We facilitate the workshops and interviews, provide the frame for cost and capacity views, and take on the drafting and writing. The team supplies business knowledge and numbers; we turn them into a coherent study.

Independent challenge

Unbiased stress-testing of assumptions: forgotten run costs, unconfirmed capacities, ignored dependencies — the questions a busy internal team rarely gets to ask itself.

Decision-quality finish

The study and its gate materials are edited into a document management can actually decide on — and the study lead is coached throughout, so the know-how stays in the team.

Why selected projects, not all of them

Hands-on support concentrates where it changes the most: projects at or near their start, where the feasibility study still shapes the decision. Projects already further along have passed that window — for them, the templates, checklists and trained colleagues coming out of Stream 2 are the right vehicle. And crucially: the project teams exist and stay in the lead. We extend their capacity and know-how compared to today — we do not take the work away from them.

Our guardrails

We carry workload, never the verdict. The business content, the numbers and the go / no-go decision remain fully VigRe's — our signature is on the quality, structure and completeness of the study. And our involvement is designed to shrink: the first study is guided closely; by the next one, the team leads and we support.

The gap log — Stream 1's second deliverable

Alongside the studies themselves, we keep a structured gap log: every point where the current way of preparing projects diverges from good practice — missing inputs, unclear roles, unconfirmed capacities, undefined decision rights. Nothing academic; one live list, maintained as we work. It is the factual, evidence-based bridge into Stream 2 — the improvement backlog is not somebody's opinion, it is what the real projects showed.

04 · Stream 2 in detail

Turning the lessons into VigRe's own standard

Stream 2 begins with a single, low-commitment step: after the first pilot study is completed, we sit down together over the gap log and decide what is worth fixing systematically. What follows is shaped by that review — the outline below shows the full possible scope.

Possible delivery content

  • Stage definition — what a feasibility study is and is not at VigRe, which initiatives need one and in what depth.
  • Feasibility dimensions — the checklist of what every study examines: business rationale, technical fit, economics, operational impact, internal capacity, risk and compliance, schedule and delivery approach.
  • Gate model and roles — the decision moment and its outcomes, plus a clear RACI: who sponsors, who leads, who contributes, who decides.
  • Templates and artefacts — study document, gate decision paper, completeness checklist, cost-model and capacity-view skeletons — seeded from the pilot studies, so they fit reality on day one.
  • Training and handover — working sessions for study leads and contributors, so the standard is a capability, not a document.

What VigRe gains

  • One language, uniform quality — every initiative examined and presented the same way, with clear expectations for everyone involved.
  • Scale without external help — the standard equips all teams across the wave; hands-on support is not needed eighteen times.
  • A clean handover into delivery — study outputs feed straight into project initiation, so approved projects never start from zero.
  • A standard that fits — because it was distilled from VigRe's real projects, not imported from a textbook shelf.
05 · To validate together

Hypotheses we start from — and questions to verify

This outline is built on our current understanding. Before shaping anything further, we want to validate the following openly — corrections are welcome and will make the approach more precise.

What state is the project wave actually in? Which of the initiatives are genuinely before or at their start, which are already in flight — and where is the feasibility gap felt most urgently right now?

Which one or two projects are the right pilots? Our suggested criteria: at or near the start, a team willing to work this way, and ideally two different types of initiative so the lessons cover the breadth of the portfolio.

Is "feasibility study" an established term at VigRe today? Do different departments mean the same thing by it — same scope, same outputs, same decision rights — or does each initiative interpret it its own way?

Is internal capacity part of feasibility today? Are the availability of internal experts, IT capacity and business involvement assessed before an initiative is committed — and if not, where has this hurt most recently?

Who should own the standard and the gate? Which role or body at VigRe is the natural owner of the feasibility standard, and who realistically sits at the gate decision?

Appendix

What a feasibility study is — reference

Note on sources: everything in this appendix reflects generally recognised good practice as described by established international standards — PMI / PMBOK, PRINCE2 and the Stage-Gate® model — not a consultant's private view of what a feasibility study should contain. The standard developed in Stream 2 would adapt this shared body of practice to VigRe's scale, governance and vocabulary.
Definition and position in the project lifecycle

A feasibility study (also "initiation study") is a structured pre-project examination of a proposed initiative, answering one question: is this worth doing, in this form, now — and under what conditions? It sits between the initial idea or funding decision and formal project initiation.

Its output is a decision paper for a gate: a recommendation of go / no-go / go-with-conditions, together with a validated scope outline, cost and benefit estimate, capacity view, key risks and a proposed delivery approach. If the decision is "go", the study's content flows directly into the project charter — the project does not start from a blank page.

Accuracy expectations are honest: at this stage a cost estimate of ±30–50 % is normal and acceptable. The point is not precision — it is that all relevant dimensions have been examined and the decision is taken consciously.

The dimensions a study examines

Established practice (the classic TELOS model, extended by organisational and capacity views) examines an initiative from these angles:

DimensionKey question
Business / strategicWhat problem does this solve, how does it link to strategy, what are the benefits, and what are the options — including "do nothing"?
TechnicalCan it be built and integrated with reasonable risk? Does it fit the architecture?
EconomicWhat is the total cost of ownership — including run costs after delivery — and does the benefit justify it?
Operational / organisationalWhat does it change for processes and people, and is the organisation ready to absorb it?
Capacity & resourcesAre the internal experts, IT capacity and business involvement actually available in the required period?
Legal / compliance / riskWhat are the regulatory implications and the key risks, and can they be managed?
Schedule & deliveryIs the timeline realistic, what does it depend on, and what delivery model fits (internal / external, iterative / plan-driven)?
Anchoring in recognised standards
  • Stage-Gate® (R. G. Cooper) — treats each gate as an investment decision about the next tranche of money; the feasibility gate is the last low-cost point to stop or redirect an initiative. The most management-intuitive framing.
  • PRINCE2 — the "Starting Up a Project" process produces an outline business case and project brief that must pass a gate before project initiation is even funded; feasibility work is deliberately separated from delivery.
  • PMI / PMBOK — places business case development and benefits analysis in the pre-project phase, owned by the sponsor, as the basis for project authorisation.
What a feasibility study is not
  • Not a detailed analysis or specification — that belongs to the project itself; the study stays at decision-making depth.
  • Not a vendor selection — it may outline sourcing options, but procurement is a separate step after approval.
  • Not an alibi — "we have a study, so we proceed" defeats the purpose; the study exists so that a no or a not-yet is a legitimate, respected outcome.
  • Not a one-size ritual — the depth of the study scales with the size and risk of the initiative; small initiatives deserve a light version, not an exemption.
